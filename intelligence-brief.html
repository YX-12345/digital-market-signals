Build or Buy? Access to AI Compute and Cloud Infrastructure in Emerging Economies

20 November 2026 · Selected cases in Asia, Africa and Latin America

Bottom Line

Start with the workload. Buying cloud services is a practical first option when demand is uncertain and the required services are available on acceptable terms. A shared compute program can address an access gap when qualified users need accelerators they cannot readily procure. Building local capacity warrants a closer look when sustained demand, power and operating capacity can be demonstrated. These are decision criteria drawn from selected cases and the World Bank’s AI foundations framework.[1]

Three Key Developments

  1. Commercial cloud providers added local regions. Microsoft made Indonesia Central generally available in April 2025 with three availability zones. Google announced that its Querétaro region had opened in December 2024. Both give buyers named locations to evaluate for local workloads.[2] [3]
  2. India launched a subsidized access channel. In March 2025 the government announced the AI Compute Portal, with initial access to 10,000 GPUs and 8,693 more planned. The announcement describes the program’s rollout and does not establish realized availability or utilization.[4]
  3. A proposed Kenyan campus remains a planning case. In May 2024 Microsoft and G42 announced a package that included a proposed geothermal-powered campus in Olkaria. The initial US$1 billion referred to the wider initiative. In May 2026 Reuters relayed Bloomberg reporting on a delay related to payment guarantees and said it could not independently verify the report.[5] [6]

Supporting Evidence

The documents answer different questions. Microsoft’s availability notice establishes that customers could select a region, while India’s ministry describes a program intended to allocate access. The Olkaria announcement sets out an investment proposal with an operating timeline conditional on definitive agreements. None gives comparable data on accelerators available to a particular buyer, actual GPU-hour prices or use. In procurement, treat the first two as options to investigate and keep the proposed campus outside any near-term delivery assumption.[2] [4] [5]

Market and Policy Trade-offs

For a cloud purchase, price the workload across compute, data movement, recovery and exit. For a shared service, define allocation rules and measure whether users can secure capacity when they need it. For a local build, test power availability, expected utilization and operating costs, including who bears demand risk. Assess data governance separately for each option. Brazil’s Resolution 19/2024, for example, sets out mechanisms for international personal data transfers, which makes the actual processing and access arrangements relevant to the review.[1] [5] [7]

Implications for Development Practitioners

Specify a pilot workload and test it against available services before recommending infrastructure investment. Record the performance target, permitted data locations, required accelerator type, and budget. Where a shared program is proposed, track the time between application and a successful allocation alongside the cost per completed workload. Use those observations to determine whether the binding constraint is price, access, connectivity, skills or something else.[1] [4]

What to Watch

Look for named accelerator types and quotas in local catalogs, published allocation outcomes for shared programs, and confirmed financing or commissioning milestones for proposed campuses. These checks would show whether the options described here are becoming usable for specific organizations.[2] [4] [5]

Sources and Limitations

The cases were selected to illustrate different routes to compute. They cannot establish which route delivers better value. Operator releases document what operators reported, a government release documents what it announced, and the later Kenya status rests on attributed reporting. Procurement implications are this brief’s analysis.
