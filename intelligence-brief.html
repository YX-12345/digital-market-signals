Build or Buy? Access to AI Compute and Cloud Infrastructure in Emerging Economies

20 November 2026 · Selected cases in Asia, Africa and Latin America

Executive takeaway

Start with a defined workload. Test commercial cloud when demand is uncertain or delivery is urgent, and test a shared program if eligible users face a documented access gap. Consider a local build when sustained demand, firm power and operating capacity can be demonstrated. Check the availability and delivery terms of the actual accelerator and services in every option.[1] [8]

Three key findings

  1. Commercial launches expand the locations buyers can evaluate. Microsoft announced general availability for Indonesia Central and Google announced the opening of Querétaro. These disclosures support testing a local option. They do not establish the quota, price or accelerator type a buyer will obtain.[2] [3]
  2. Shared compute introduces a different route to access. India launched an AI Compute Portal and reported initial access to 10,000 GPUs with further capacity planned. Eligibility, wait times and completed jobs are the measures a team needs to judge actual access.[4]
  3. Physical capacity follows a project pipeline. USTDA funded feasibility work for two Nigerian facilities. In Bihar, the October operator disclosure and November reporting identify different locations for proposals of the same stated scale. Neither supports including new operating capacity in a delivery plan.[9] [10] [11]

Market and policy trade-offs

General availability establishes that a cloud region can be selected. It takes a workload test to establish usable services, accelerator quotas, pricing and data-location compliance. A shared-compute launch creates an application route, while allocation, queue time and support determine actual access. Local investment moves through financing, site and power commitments, construction and commissioning. An operational site still needs to demonstrate hardware availability and service terms for the intended workload.

Questions to take into due diligence

  • Which workload, accelerator, software stack, service level and permitted data locations are required? Can a pilot meet those conditions at an acceptable total cost?
  • What access is demonstrable today? Ask for regional catalog and quota confirmation, or for figures on allocation waits and jobs completed by eligible users.
  • Who bears demand, power and delivery risk if new capacity is built? Ask for dated evidence of site control, firm power and commissioning plans. For affected transactions, verify licensing and contract obligations. For personal data, confirm the applicable rules, including transfer mechanisms where relevant.[7] [8]

What to watch

Track updated accelerator listings and delivery terms in cloud regions, reported allocations from shared programs, and evidence of financing, grid connection and commissioning for proposed facilities. Treat changes in export-control guidance as separate from changes in available supply.[8]

Country infrastructure context

The Digital Infrastructure Dashboard compares internet use, fixed broadband and electricity access. These country indicators inform questions about users and service delivery conditions. Evaluate accelerator availability, usable cloud services and facility power using the project- and provider-level evidence above.

Evidence and limitations

Source checks establish what an operator or government agency disclosed and what the cited rule says. Plans and reported delays require milestone checks. For example, Olkaria was announced as a proposal, and the later delay was attributed reporting.[5] [6] The comparison and questions are independent portfolio analysis.
